\paragraph{Paris and Amsterdam.} The margin between \MLSHeadlineWinner{} and \MLSHeadlineLoser{} is small
compared with the spread of the full ranking, and the two cities win on different ingredients
(Table~\ref{tab:h2h}). \MLSHeadlineWinner{}'s lead comes mainly from observation density. Within that component,
the iNaturalist record rates per km\textsuperscript{2} of the two cities are close; the difference lies in the
fungal share of all records and, above all, in GBIF records per km\textsuperscript{2}, where \MLSHeadlineWinner{}
is far ahead. GBIF depth reflects which recording schemes publish their data, so this part of the gap describes
recording and publication effort as much as fungal life. \MLSHeadlineWinner{} also gains on species richness and
on the climate index. \MLSHeadlineLoser{} recovers points on seasonal reliability, because its records are spread
over more months, whereas \MLSHeadlineWinner{}'s records are concentrated in a short autumn peak. It also recovers
points on mapped woodland.

\paragraph{The leaders.} The highest-ranked European locations (Figure~\ref{fig:contrib}) score strongly on
at least two of the three record-based ingredients: observation density, species richness and seasonal
reliability. Climate
suitability does not set them apart. \MLSLeaderName{}, the overall leader, and the Nordic capital that follows it
receive fewer climate points than most of the field, while the climate index favours the mild Atlantic locations,
several of which rank in the lower half. Under this score, being a place where many fungi are recorded across
many months matters more than having a favourable climate on paper. Because the leaders are large, well-observed
cities, this pattern is consistent with the observer-effort caveat below, and the score should be read as
``recorded fungal life'', not as abundance.

\paragraph{Canada.} The Canadian benchmarks span almost the whole range of the ranking.
\MLSBestBenchmarkName{} is placed at rank~\MLSBestBenchmarkRank{} overall, the two British Columbia locations hold
the highest species-richness scores of all locations, and Ottawa also ranks among the leaders, while Calgary ranks
last. Halifax, Montreal, Quebec City and Toronto fall in the lower half of the table, all with climate points below
the European median. The European
headline cities therefore sit below the best Canadian benchmarks but above most of the others.

\paragraph{Caveats specific to this run.} Two data limitations shape these results (Section~\ref{sec:coverage}).
First, the WorldPop statistics service was unavailable, so the per-capita observation rate was dropped for all
locations; the density component in this run rests on area rates, fungal share and GBIF depth only. Second, the
woodland share counts only woodland that the Overpass API exposes as an area, which in practice means named
multipolygons. It is therefore a lower bound whose bias varies with local mapping practice: several plainly
forested locations, including Trento and Victoria, receive no habitat points. Cross-region comparisons of the
habitat ingredient should not be relied on, but it carries the smallest weight in the score.
